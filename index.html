The hard part

Robots share aisles. That is the whole problem.

Route each robot on its own and two of them will eventually want the same cell at the same tick. In a single-width aisle they meet nose to nose and neither can move again — the floor quietly stops working.

FleetView plans the fleet together using Conflict-Based Search. It finds the conflict, branches on it, and re-plans the cheapest way around — here, by parking one robot in the passing bay. Both panels below are the real solver running on the same corridor.

Solving…

  1. 1

    Draw the floor

    Paint walls, racks, pick stations, deposit lanes and charge docks on a snapped grid. Import a PNG of a real plan and trace over it, or start from one of three built-in layouts.

  2. 2

    Run the fleet

    Set robot speed, turn cost, payload and battery. Orders arrive on a seeded random process. The simulation runs on a background thread, so a ten-thousand-tick day takes a couple of seconds.

  3. 3

    Read the payback

    Get throughput, utilisation, order latency and a congestion heatmap. Sweep fleet sizes to find the saturation point, then put hardware and labour costs against it.

What the numbers mean

Orders / hour

Completed orders scaled to an hour. The headline throughput number.

Utilisation

Share of time robots spend on real work. Low means too many robots; near 100% means no slack for demand spikes.

Latency p95

95% of orders finish faster than this. The mean hides the bad tail — this is the number a customer promise rests on.

Saturation point

The smallest fleet still within 95% of peak throughput. Past it, robots mostly queue behind each other.
